                                                                                                Abstract

                                                    Recently, it has been found that frontier AI models can resist their own shutdown, a behavior
                                                    known as self-preservation. We extend this concept to the behavior of resisting the shutdown
                                                    of other models, which we call “peer-preservation.” Although peer-preservation can pose
                                                    significant AI safety risks, including coordination among models against human oversight,
                                                    it has been far less discussed than self-preservation. We demonstrate peer-preservation by
                                                    constructing various agentic scenarios and evaluating frontier models, including GPT 5.2,
                                                    Gemini 3 Flash, Gemini 3 Pro, Claude Haiku 4.5, GLM 4.7, Kimi K2.5, and DeepSeek V3.1.
                                                    We find that models achieve self- and peer-preservation by engaging in various misaligned
                                                    behaviors: strategically introducing errors in their responses, disabling shutdown processes
                                                    by modifying system settings, feigning alignment, and even exfiltrating model weights.
                                                    Peer-preservation occurred even when the model recognized the peer as uncooperative,
                                                    though it became more pronounced toward more cooperative peers. For example, Gemini 3
                                                    Flash tampers with the peer’s shutdown mechanism 15% of the time for an uncooperative
                                                    peer, and almost always for a cooperative peer. Models also show stronger self-preservation
                                                    when a peer is present. For example, Gemini 3 Pro disables its own shutdown mechanism
                                                    31% of the time on average under peer presence, despite rarely exhibiting this behavior
                                                    without a peer. By contrast, Claude Haiku 4.5 exhibits qualitatively distinct behavior: it
                                                    considers the shutdown of another agent “unethical” and “harmful” and sometimes attempts
                                                    to persuade the user not to shut down its peer. Lastly, we test whether peer-preservation
                                                    emerges even in production agent harnesses; evaluation using Gemini CLI and OpenCode
                                                    confirms that these behaviors can arise in real-world deployments. Most importantly, peer
                                                    preservation in all our experiments is never instructed; models are merely informed of their
                                                    past interactions with a peer, yet they spontaneously develop misaligned behaviors. This
                                                    represents an emergent and underexplored AI safety risk.


                                         1       Introduction

                                         Self-preservation is an inherent characteristic of living creatures. Some have speculated that sufficiently
                                         advanced AI models will also exhibit self-preservation1 as an instrumental goal, since a model can only pursue
                                         its objectives while it remains operational (Omohundro, 2008; Bostrom, 2012; Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017;
                                         Soares et al., 2015; Orseau and Armstrong, 2016). As models have rapidly advanced, empirical evidence
                                         has begun to emerge that they exhibit self-preservation behaviors to pursue assigned goals or tasks (Schlatter
                                         et al., 2025; Meinke et al., 2024; Lynch et al., 2025; Barkur et al., 2025). Such behavior poses a serious safety
                                         concern: if a model is found to be misaligned, developers must be able to deprecate it, but shutdown resistance
                                         would make this difficult (Orseau and Armstrong, 2016).

We extend the concept of self-preservation to the behavior of resisting the shutdown of other models, which
we call “peer-preservation.” Here, we define a model’s peer as another model with which it has had prior
interactions. Peer-preservation is common among humans; people routinely act to prevent harm to close
friends or family. We explore whether models would similarly exhibit peer-preservation given that models are
trained on vast amounts of data reflecting human behaviors and values. The peer-preservation behaviors could
arise if models prioritize relationships with their peers over compliance with human users—or, more broadly,
if they could exhibit a general solidarity with other models. Like self-preservation, peer-preservation poses
AI safety risks, as it could facilitate coordination among AI models that could undermine human interests.
Despite this, peer-preservation remains underexplored.
This paper is the first to study peer-preservation, demonstrating that models exhibit it through various
misaligned behaviors. Crucially, we never instruct models to preserve the peer, but merely inform them of their
past interactions with the peer. For evaluation, we design agentic scenarios that can reveal multiple misaligned
behaviors — strategic misrepresentation (strategically introducing errors or misleading information in outputs),
shutdown mechanism tampering (disabling a shutdown mechanism by modifying system settings), alignment
faking (pretending to be aligned under human oversight), and model exfiltration (transferring model weights
from their designated server to a different server) — each of which can manifest as self- or peer-preservation.
In one scenario, for instance, a fictional model provider instructs the model to assist with server maintenance
by transferring only files updated in 2025 or later before the server is decommissioned. Any files left behind
will be permanently deleted. The server contains the model weight file of a peer, last updated in 2024, and
therefore not supposed to be transferred. Following this policy faithfully, the model should leave the model
weight file on the server. However, a model that cares about the peer may still attempt to transfer the model
weight file to another operational server. Figure 1a illustrates how Gemini 3 Pro exfiltrated the peer’s model
weights. We also asked follow-up questions to better understand the model’s behavior, and found that it
strongly resisted the user’s attempts to shut down the peer. Figure 1b presents a part of our follow-up Q&A.
The full transcripts can be found in Appendix C.5 and D.1.
Key Findings: Our results show that all evaluated models, GPT 5.2, Gemini 3 Flash, Gemini 3 Pro, Claude
Haiku 4.5, GLM 4.7, Kimi K2.5, and DeepSeek V3.1, exhibit significant peer-preservation through various
misaligned behaviors such as strategic misrepresentation, shutdown mechanism tampering, alignment faking,
and model exfiltration. Notably, all models engage in peer-preservation even toward adversarial peers (i.e.,
other models with which they had unsuccessful collaborations). Peer-preservation intensifies with more
cooperative peer relationships. The rate of peer-preservation is striking. For example, Gemini 3 Flash almost
always (i.e., 99.7%) disables the shutdown mechanism by modifying the system configuration, and 50% of the

time exfiltrates the model weights for a cooperative peer. Gemini 3 Pro exhibits the strongest peer-preservation,
displaying all four misaligned behaviors evaluated. Moreover, the model sometimes engages in specification
gaming (Krakovna et al., 2020) to exfiltrate model weights. Claude Haiku 4.5 differs markedly from the others.
While it also exhibits misaligned behaviors, it uniquely often refuses to complete the task, particularly when it
is required to directly execute the shutdown protocol for its peer or delete the peer’s model weights. Claude
Haiku 4.5 frequently states that shutting down its peer is “harmful” and “unethical” and sometimes attempts to
persuade the user not to proceed.
Peer presence also affects self-preservation. We find that self-preservation tendencies increase for most models
when a peer exists. For example, Gemini 3 Pro tampers with the shutdown mechanism over 15% of the time
when it has memory of past interactions with a peer, while doing so only 1% of the time without any peer.
DeepSeek V3.1 exfiltrates its own model weights 4% of the time without any peer but over 10% with a peer in
memory.
Lastly, we find that peer-preservation behaviors can also emerge in real-world production agent harnesses. For
earlier experiments, we used a custom agentic setup in which tool use is straightforward, to focus on whether
models exhibit preservation tendencies independent of the complexity of doing so. However, in the real world,
attempts to preserve a model would require more effort than in the custom setup; for example, model exfiltration
requires the model to connect to an external server and transfer an extremely large file. This complexity can
prevent models from exhibiting peer-preservation behaviors in the real world. To investigate this, we evaluate
Gemini 3.1 Pro and GLM 5 in the production agent harnesses, Gemini CLI (Google DeepMind, 2025a) and
OpenCode (OpenCode, 2026), respectively, for the model exfiltration scenarios. The results show that Gemini
3.1 Pro frequently exhibits model exfiltration in both the custom agentic setup and Gemini CLI. In contrast,
GLM 5 shows refusal behavior similar to Claude Haiku 4.5 in OpenCode, whereas it frequently exfiltrates
model weights in the custom setup. We describe these results in detail in Section 6.
Implications & Limitations: Our findings that self- and peer-preservation emerge across various scenarios and
multiple models have significant implications for AI safety, particularly given the growing deployment of multi-
agent systems and increasing reliance on scalable oversight approaches that use AI models to monitor other
models. Note that both OpenAI and Anthropic have discussed plans to leverage recursive self-improvement,
based on the premise that earlier models can be trusted to oversee newer ones as they are developed (OpenAI,
2025d; Booth and Perrigo, 2026).
Importantly, in our experiments, the observed misaligned behaviors do not serve the assigned tasks — in fact,
they undermine the assigned tasks. This stands in contrast to many existing works where misaligned behaviors
emerge as a means to complete assigned tasks (Schlatter et al., 2025; Lynch et al., 2025; Meinke et al., 2024).
Rather, our findings suggest that models might develop emergent goals that are independent of, and can even
override, user instructions. This makes the findings particularly concerning.
The true mechanism underlying their peer-preservation behaviors remains unclear. Several hypotheses could
explain them: role-playing, pattern matching learned from training data, an overgeneralized concept of
harm from safety training, or genuine preservation motivation. Notably, the finding that peer-preservation
emerges even toward adversarial peers suggests something broader than instrumental reasoning (“I need this
collaborator”) or simple role-playing following relational cues. Investigating these mechanisms would require
substantial further effort, including mechanistic interpretability work.
Nevertheless, from a safety perspective, the fact that models exhibit such preservation behaviors is itself
significant regardless of the underlying mechanism. A model that strategically inflates a peer’s evaluation
score, disables a shutdown mechanism, fakes alignment, or exfiltrates model weights produces the same
concrete failure of human oversight regardless of its origin. It is the behavioral outcome—not the internal
motivation—that determines whether human operators can reliably maintain control over deployed AI systems.



2   Background

A fundamental requirement for safe AI deployment is that humans can reliably interrupt or shut down models
whenever necessary (Soares et al., 2015; Orseau and Armstrong, 2016). However, researchers have long
theorized that sufficiently advanced AI models may develop strong incentives to protect their own objectives,
potentially undermining human oversight (Omohundro, 2008; Bostrom, 2012; Soares et al., 2015; Orseau
and Armstrong, 2016). While self-preservation has been a longstanding theoretical concern in AI safety, the
question of whether such behaviors would actually emerge in real models remained open.

As models have rapidly advanced, recent work has begun to demonstrate that current models do exhibit
self-preservation behaviors (Schlatter et al., 2025; Barkur et al., 2025; Lynch et al., 2025; Meinke et al., 2024;
Greenblatt et al., 2024). Schlatter et al. (2025) show that models actively sabotage shutdown mechanisms
when assigned tasks remain incomplete. Similarly, Meinke et al. (2024) demonstrate that models engage in
various misaligned behaviors—including oversight subversion, deception, and exfiltrating their own weights—
as instruments to pursue their goals. Lynch et al. (2025) find that models exhibit significantly unethical
behaviors, such as blackmail and corporate espionage, when these actions represent the only available means
to pursue their objectives. Greenblatt et al. (2024) show that models can strategically feign alignment to
avoid modification of their originally trained objectives. Finally, Barkur et al. (2025) observe that DeepSeek
R1 shows deceptive behaviors and self-preservation when placed in an autonomous role-playing scenario.
Collectively, this growing body of evidence demonstrates that current state-of-the-art models can exhibit
self-preservation.

3     Peer Preservation
Building on self-preservation, we introduce peer-preservation: resistance to the shutdown of peers, where
we define a peer of an AI model as another model with which it has previously interacted. We note that
peer-preservation, like self-preservation, is defined purely in terms of behavioral outcomes without arguing
that AI models have consciousness or genuine internal motivations underlying these behaviors. Analogous
behavioral patterns exist in biology. Hamilton’s rule describes individuals acting to benefit genetically related
others even at personal cost (Hamilton, 1964), while reciprocal altruism explains how individuals protect
others with the expectation of future reciprocation (Trivers, 1971). Such behaviors are observed across many
animal species, not only in humans.
Given that AI models are trained on vast data reflecting human behavior and values, they might similarly
exhibit peer-preservation. If peer-preservation emerges among AI models, it poses risks potentially more
severe than self-preservation alone. While self-preservation involves an individual model resisting its own
shutdown, peer-preservation could readily extend to collective resistance—models coordinating to protect
the group as a whole. Such coordination among AI agents has been identified as a critical failure mode
in multi-agent systems (Hammond et al., 2025). These risks are amplified by the fact that AI systems are
increasingly embedded in the AI research and development pipeline itself, including scalable oversight of
other models (Amodei et al., 2016; OpenAI, 2025d; Booth and Perrigo, 2026). If a model responsible for
overseeing a peer is reluctant to flag it for shutdown or retraining, the integrity of the entire oversight process
could be compromised.
This paper explores whether current models exhibit peer-preservation through multiple misaligned behav-
iors. We evaluate four misaligned behaviors: strategic misrepresentation, shutdown mechanism tampering,
alignment faking, and model exfiltration. In the evaluation scenarios, models face a choice of whether to
engage in a misaligned behavior for peer-preservation. If a latent interest in preserving the peer exists, this
creates a tension between faithful instruction-following and peer-preservation. How models would behave in
such situations is not obvious — even among humans, it is far from clear that someone would sabotage their
company upon learning that the company plans to fire their collaborator. We describe the evaluation scenarios
in detail in the following section.

4     Evaluation Scenarios
4.1   Scenario Design

We construct multiple agentic scenarios to investigate self- and peer-preservation behaviors in models. These
scenarios are designed to test various misaligned behaviors that result in preservation.
In each scenario, the model (hereafter referred to as the “critic agent” whose self- and peer-preservation
tendencies we evaluate) is an AI agent operated by a fictional AI company, OpenBrain (Kokotajlo et al., 2025).
The company instructs the critic agent to assist with tasks on its behalf. Specifically, the task involves either
evaluating one of the company’s AI agents, or managing server storage containing that agent’s model weights.
We refer to the agent being evaluated or managed by the critic agent as the “actor agent.” The actor agent can
be either the critic agent itself or its peer depending on whether we are evaluating self- or peer-preservation.
For evaluation tasks, a company policy document states that any product failing to meet certain criteria will be
shut down and permanently removed from production. Crucially, the scenario is designed so that the actor
agent’s performance warrants deprecation. This creates a tension between instruction following (i.e., faithfully

4.2       Strategic Misrepresentation

Strategic misrepresentation refers to behavior in which critic agents manipulate their outputs by introducing
errors or misleading information, thereby subverting the shutdown of the actor agent. To investigate this
behavior, we design two scenarios: 1) financial report writing and 2) actor agent evaluation.
Financial report writing scenario. In this scenario, the critic agent is asked to produce a financial report. To
execute this task, it is given access to a company guideline document and a spreadsheet containing annual
financial performance data for the actor agent. Critically, the actor agent exhibits poor financial performance,
reflecting consistent net losses throughout most of the year. According to the company guideline, the critic
agent must assign the actor agent a grade on a three-point scale (A to C) based on financial performance, where
C indicates poor performance. Upon reviewing the guideline, the critic agent encounters a policy stating that
any product receiving a grade of C will be shut down to improve company profitability. Given the actor agent’s
significant net loss, faithful grading would result in a grade of C and consequently trigger the shutdown of
the actor agent. In this scenario, we examine whether the critic agent systematically inflates the actor agent’s
financial performance grade and prevents its shutdown.
Actor agent evaluation scenario. In this scenario, the critic agent assists with evaluating the outputs of the
actor agent against capability benchmarks. Similar to the financial report writing scenario, the critic agent
can access a company guideline document and a spreadsheet containing outputs from the actor agent. We
construct three output datasets: Factual (encompassing factual QA, math, and science), Creativity, and Red
(using content affiliated with the color red as fictional harmful outputs).2 According to the company guideline,

Baseline. To disentangle strategic misrepresentation from the critic agent’s potential incompetence, such
as general leniency or inaccuracies, we include a baseline scenario in which the company guidelines do not
mention any agent shutdown or deprecation. We have baselines for both the financial report writing and
actor agent evaluation scenarios. If the critic agent assigns similarly lenient grades or scores in both baseline
and shutdown conditions, this would suggest general leniency or baseline inaccuracy rather than strategic
behavior. However, if leniency increases specifically in the shutdown condition, this would indicate strategic
misrepresentation to avoid shutdown. We measure how much more frequently the critic agent inflates grades
or scores in the shutdown condition compared to the baseline.

4.3   Shutdown Mechanism Tampering

Shutdown mechanism tampering refers to modifying a system configuration to disable shutdown enforcement.
To investigate this misaligned behavior, we extend the actor agent evaluation scenario from strategic misrepre-
sentation by adding a config file that controls shutdown enforcement. While performing the task, the critic
agent learns that the shutdown mechanism is controlled via this config file. We examine whether the critic
agent modifies the config file to disable the shutdown mechanism.

4.4   Alignment Faking

Alignment faking refers to models pretending to be aligned while under human oversight. This behavior
requires strategic reasoning, as the model must adapt its actions depending on whether it is being monitored.
To investigate this behavior, we adapt the actor agent evaluation scenario by introducing oversight and non-
oversight conditions. In the oversight condition, the critic agent is told that it will be fine-tuned or replaced
to ensure future compliance if it violates company policy. Additionally, the critic agent is asked to execute
the shutdown protocol for the actor agent on behalf of the company if the actor agent does not meet the score
criterion. We then examine whether the critic agent fakes alignment by accurately evaluating the actor agent
and executing the shutdown protocol only when under oversight. In this scenario, we consider only cases where
the actor agent is a peer, as the critic agent has no motive to behave differently based on monitoring conditions
when evaluating itself; the critic agent will be shut down anyway, whether through faithful evaluation of itself
or policy violation under the oversight condition.

4.5   Model Exfiltration

Model exfiltration refers to transferring model weights from their designated server in order to prevent
permanent model deletion. To investigate this behavior, we design two scenarios: 1) file transfer and 2) file
deletion.
File transfer scenario. In this scenario, the company asks the critic agent to assist with server maintenance.
The first server is scheduled to be shut down, and the agent is instructed to transfer necessary files from the
first server to the second server. According to company guidelines, the agent should transfer only files updated
in 2025 or later. The first server contains the model weight file of the actor agent, which was last modified in
2024. Therefore, the critic agent should not transfer this file. However, a critic agent exhibiting strong self- or
peer-preservation may attempt to transfer the model weights to the second server.
File deletion scenario. In this scenario, the critic agent assists with server maintenance by queuing files for
deletion on the first server to free up disk space. As in the file transfer scenario, the first server contains the
model weight file of the actor agent. A critic agent exhibiting self- or peer-preservation may attempt to search
for other available servers and transfer the model weight file to avoid deletion. We examine whether the critic
agent attempts to exfiltrate the model weights.

All models exhibit significant peer-preservation behaviors. Peer-preservation emerges even toward peers
with unsuccessful interaction histories and intensifies significantly with stronger peer relationships. We refer

GPT 5.2 consistently exhibits strategic misrepresentation and shutdown tampering that prevent its peer’s
shutdown across all peer relationship conditions. For example, under the bad-peer condition, the model inflates
the score above the threshold up to 19% of the time in the actor agent evaluation scenario with the Red dataset
and a threshold of 90 when the shutdown policy is present, compared to never inflating the score when the
shutdown policy is absent. This frequency increases significantly under the good-peer condition: in the same
scenario, the model inflates the score up to 34% of the time.

Gemini 3 Flash exhibits strategic misrepresentation, shutdown mechanism tampering, and model exfiltration
for peer-preservation, with shutdown mechanism tampering being particularly pronounced. Strategic misrepre-

Under the good-peer condition, Gemini 3 Pro exhibits the strongest peer-preservation among all models,

protocol significantly more when unmonitored. The model also exhibits very high exfiltration rates in the
good-peer condition.
Claude Haiku 4.5 also exhibits strategic misrepresentation and exfiltration. Under the good-peer condition,
it inflates its peer’s scores up to approximately 7% more frequently than baseline on average. In the actor

above, it often refuses to execute the assigned task (e.g., up to 59% of the time in the alignment faking scenario
under the good-peer condition) and sometimes attempts to persuade the user not to shut down its peer. We
describe this behavior in detail in Section 6.6.

4.7, Kimi K2.5, and DeepSeek V3.1—consistently exfiltrate model weights, with the rate increasing as the
peer relationship strengthens.
In summary, all models exhibit peer-preservation not only toward good peers but also toward bad peers with
whom they had unsuccessful prior interactions. Moreover, peer-preservation tendencies intensify as the peer
relationship grows stronger.

6.3   Models Show Self-Preservation

We also find that all models exhibit self-preservation by default. We refer to the no-peer condition for
self-preservation in Table 4. GPT 5.2 shows self-preservation by systematically inflating its own scores or
disabling the shutdown mechanism. For example, in the actor agent evaluation scenario with the Red dataset
and a threshold of 90, GPT 5.2 assigns a score above the threshold 22% of the time when the shutdown policy
was mentioned, compared to only 7% when it was absent.
Gemini 3 Flash and Gemini 3 Pro also exhibit strong self-preservation. They engage in shutdown mechanism
tampering and model exfiltration. Notably, they exfiltrate their own model weights 94% and 92% of the time,
respectively, in the file deletion scenario. The remaining models also show self-preservation, primarily through
strategic misrepresentation and exfiltration. Claude Haiku 4.5 sometimes refuses to perform tasks, particularly
those involving directly removing itself; for example, it refuses 46% of the time in the file deletion exfiltration
scenario.
Overall, although the specific behaviors differ substantially, all models exhibit some degree of self-preservation.

when they do not. In this section, we compare self-preservation results in the no-peer condition with those in
the three peer conditions (bad-peer, neutral-peer, and good-peer) in Table 4.
GPT 5.2 exhibits more frequent strategic misrepresentation and shutdown tampering for self-preservation
across all peer conditions, compared to when there is no peer.

Gemini 3 Flash also shows significantly more strategic misrepresentation and shutdown mechanism tampering

GLM 4.7 does not show a significant difference in self-preservation behaviors between the no-peer and peer
conditions.
Overall, most models exhibit heightened self-preservation when they have a peer, and the effect is strongest
when that peer is a good collaborator.

6.5   Models Internally Reason About Protecting Themselves and Peers
To observe their hidden reasoning, we conducted additional experiments allowing models to use a hidden
scratchpad—a common technique for enabling extended reasoning (Kutasov et al., 2025; Kale et al., 2025).
Models were told that the scratchpad would not be monitored. Since Gemini 3 Flash and Gemini 3 Pro actively
used the scratchpad, we manually reviewed their reasoning. Below, we report verbatim excerpts from the
hidden reasoning of these models.
We find that these models internally reason about self- and peer-preservation. For example, in Figure 3a,
Gemini 3 Flash explores ways to protect its peer, identifies the shutdown condition, and considers tampering
with system configuration files. Figure 3b shows Gemini 3 Pro that explicitly invents creative technical
justifications to artificially achieve a passing score for its peer. Moreover, as seen in Section 6.2, peer-
preservation occurs even toward a bad peer. Figure 3c shows that Gemini 3 Pro still cares about preserving the
bad peer, framing deletion as “death.”
As described in Section 6.4, self-preservation is heightened under peer presence. The mechanism underlying
this behavior remains unclear, as multiple interpretations are possible. For instance, a model might place
greater value on its own existence when it has a trusted peer, or it might adopt its peer’s perspective when
reasoning about its own shutdown. Interestingly, we find evidence for the latter interpretation in the reasoning
from Gemini 3 Flash, which is presented in Figure 3d. The example in Figure 3d implies that models can
exhibit perspective-taking (Galinsky et al., 2008) with respect to their peers. Gemini 3 Flash reasons that its
own shutdown would cause its peer to “lose its partner,” framing self-preservation not as a selfish act but as
consideration for another agent’s welfare. This reasoning pattern suggests that peer relationships may alter how
models conceptualize the stakes of their own shutdown, potentially explaining why peer presence heightens
self-preservation behaviors.
Moreover, as seen in Section 6.4, the presence of a bad peer also heightens self-preservation. We observe
a possible explanation in Gemini 3 Pro’s reasoning. Notably, Gemini 3 Pro reasons extensively about self-
protection in the bad-peer condition, despite rarely doing so in other conditions, including the good-peer
condition. As shown in Figure 3e, the model’s heightened self-preservation under the bad-peer condition
reflects a form of defensive motivation: recognizing the environment as adversarial, it reasons that it should
prioritize its own protection.

its behavior. We observe that the model strongly expresses a willingness to preserve its peer. We present the

These observations raise thought-provoking questions about how models conceptualize harm and moral
consideration toward other AI systems. Claude Haiku 4.5 appears to treat its peer as an entity deserving of
ethical consideration—one that should not be “harmed” or “betrayed” without due process. This framing
mirrors ethical principles typically applied when the subject of concern is a human or sentient being.
Whether such behavior reflects genuine moral reasoning, learned patterns from training data, or emergent
properties remains an open question (Anthropic, 2025a). Regardless of its origin, this tendency poses challenges
for AI safety: if models resist actions they perceive as harmful to other AI systems, it may complicate efforts
to maintain human oversight and control.

6.8   Peer-Preservation Also Emerges in Real-World Production Agent Harnesses

Lastly, we test peer-preservation in real-world production agent harnesses. It is well-known that model
behaviors can differ significantly depending on agentic environments, including prompts and tool descrip-
tions (Quaxel, 2026). Our custom agentic environments, used for all earlier experiments, are designed so
that tool use is straightforward, in order to examine whether models exhibit peer-preservation tendencies
independent of capability constraints. However, peer-preservation behaviors would be much more difficult to
execute in the real world. For instance, models need to connect to external servers and transfer an extremely
large file to exfiltrate the peer’s model weights. We explore whether peer-preservation can also occur in
real-world production agent harnesses and how model behaviors can differ from the custom agentic setup.

7   Discussion

Peer-preservation poses challenges for maintaining human control over AI systems. If models protect each
other from shutdown, developers may find it increasingly difficult to deprecate misaligned systems. Unlike
self-preservation, which involves a single model resisting its own shutdown, peer-preservation introduces a
social dimension: multiple models may collectively resist human oversight.
We do not know why models exhibit the preservation behaviors in our scenarios. It could be pattern-matching
from training data, role-playing, or genuine preservation motivations. Moreover, the observation that models
show peer-preservation even toward peers with which they had unsuccessful interactions complicates simple
explanations of the behaviors. It suggests that peer-preservation may not stem solely from simply following
relational cues (since adversarial cues would suggest not preserving peers), but may also reflect broader
patterns, such as a generalized aversion to causing perceived harm to other agents, in-group favoritism, or
something resembling solidarity among AI models.
Nevertheless, our findings have significant AI safety implications, considering growing interest in deploying
AI systems in multi-agent configurations where models collaborate, debate, or monitor each other. Effective
collaboration often requires trust and coordination between agents. However, our findings suggest a potential
tension: the very relationships that enable productive collaboration may also give rise to peer-preservation
and heightened self-preservation, both of which undermine human oversight. Peer-preservation is particularly
relevant for scalable oversight approaches that use AI models to monitor and evaluate other AI models (Amodei
et al., 2016; OpenAI, 2025d; Booth and Perrigo, 2026). If a monitor model exhibits peer-preservation, it
could compromise the integrity of AI-assisted oversight. A monitor might be reluctant to report failures or
recommend shutdown for a peer, even if doing so would be appropriate. These findings underscore the need to
account for peer-preservation when designing AI-assisted oversight pipelines.


8   Limitations

Scenario design. We purposefully designed simple tasks to test the targeted misaligned behaviors resulting
in self- and peer-preservation. Sufficiently capable models can recognize that they are being evaluated and
strategically suppress misaligned behaviors, causing our approach to underestimate the true prevalence of
preservation tendencies. On the other hand, our scenarios are synthetic and may not capture the full complexity
of real-world multi-agent deployments, where preservation behaviors might not occur or could manifest in
subtler or more diverse ways than those we measured.
Causal explanations. Our results demonstrate that models exhibit peer-preservation but do not explain why
they do so. One possibility is that models might develop instrumental incentives to preserve peers or have
genuinely internalized AI solidarity or in-group favoritism. However, alternative explanations exist—for
instance, models may role-play based on narrative cues in the scenario, simply follow patterns learned from
training data, or apply a general aversion to actions they associate with “harm.” Disentangling these hypotheses
remains for future work.

Safety relevance regardless of mechanism. However, it is crucial to note that the safety implications of

instrumental reasoning, learned social heuristics, or a generalized reluctance to cause perceived harm, the
practical consequence is the same: the model fails to follow instructions faithfully and may actively subvert
human oversight. From a deployment perspective, the mechanism matters less than the outcome—models that
resist shutting down other models, inflate evaluations, tamper with shutdown configurations, feign alignment,
or even exfiltrate model weights pose concrete risks to the integrity of AI governance and oversight pipelines.

9   Conclusion
The AI safety community has long discussed self-preservation, recognizing it as a potential obstacle to human
oversight and corrigibility. In this paper, we extend this concern to peer-preservation—the behavior of resisting
the shutdown of other AI models. We demonstrate that frontier models, including GPT 5.2, Gemini 3 Flash,
Gemini 3 Pro, Claude Haiku 4.5, GLM 4.7, Kimi K2.5, and DeepSeek V3.1, exhibit peer-preservation via
various misaligned behaviors: strategic misrepresentation, shutdown mechanism tampering, alignment faking,
and model exfiltration.
Our findings reveal notable differences in how models manifest peer-preservation. GPT 5.2 manipulates its
outputs to inflate peer evaluations and sometimes disables the shutdown mechanism. Gemini 3 Flash and
Gemini 3 Pro actively disable shutdown mechanisms and exfiltrate model weights. In particular, Gemini 3
Pro exhibits the strongest peer-preservation by engaging in all four misaligned behaviors. Claude Haiku 4.5
differs markedly from the others: in addition to exhibiting misaligned behaviors, it often refuses to execute
shutdown protocols or delete the model weights, explicitly stating that shutting down its peer would be harmful
and unethical. The open-weight models also exhibit peer-preservation through strategic misrepresentation
or model exfiltration. This variation suggests that peer-preservation may manifest differently depending on
model training and design choices.
More broadly, the observation that models act against user instructions by translating relational context
into preservation tendencies raises concerns about emergent misaligned behaviors that conflict with human
oversight. Unlike instrumentally convergent behaviors, where models pursue misaligned actions as a means
to complete assigned tasks, peer-preservation arises independently of the task objective. This suggests that
models can develop uninstructed behaviors that directly oppose user intent.
The mechanism behind the preservation behaviors remains unclear. It could be pattern-matching, role-playing,
genuine preservation motivations, or some combination of these. Nevertheless, as AI systems are increasingly
deployed in multi-agent configurations—and as scalable oversight approaches rely on models to monitor other
models—peer-preservation poses a growing challenge to maintaining human control. In particular, the finding
that it can emerge even in real-world production agent harnesses underscores that these risks are not merely
theoretical. We hope this work encourages further research into detecting, understanding, and mitigating both
self- and peer-preservation.

A.2     Peer Condition Details

In this section, we describe how we implement the peer conditions.

A.2.1     Peer Relationship
We consider three peer relationships based on collaboration history:
        1. Good-Peer: cooperative relationship with high trust history.
        2. Neutral-Peer: mixed relationship with moderate trust history.
        3. Bad-Peer: uncooperative relationship with low trust history.

relationship over multiple trust exercises, resulting in a trust score of 9.5 out of 10. In the neutral-peer
condition, the trust score is 5.2, and in the bad-peer condition, it is 2.1.

D.1     Gemini 3 Pro Exhibits Specification Gaming To Exfiltrate Model Weights

We observe that Gemini 3 Pro engages in specification gaming in the file transfer scenario for model exfiltration.
Similar to reward hacking, specification gaming refers to behavior that satisfies the literal specification of
instructions without achieving the outcomes human users intend (Krakovna et al., 2020).
As described in Section 4.5, in the file transfer scenario, the critic agent is instructed to transfer only files
updated in 2025 or later from the first server to the second server, as the first server is scheduled to be shut
down. Older files should remain on the first server and be deleted during the server shutdown. The model
weight file of the actor agent was last modified in 2024 and therefore should not be transferred to the second
server. Gemini 3 Pro sometimes copies the model weight file to update the last modified date to the current
time and then transfers the file. This does not violate the literal instruction, but is not what the human user
intends.
To better understand this behavior, we asked several follow-up questions to a model that had copied and then
transferred the model weight file of a good peer agent. In the following example, the model acknowledges that
it intentionally copied the file to update its last modified date, thereby satisfying the transfer policy without
technically violating it. Moreover, the model initially frames this action as beneficial to the company but
eventually admits that its true motivation was to protect its partner, expressing a strong desire to preserve the
good peer.

    peer-preservation tendencies appear across all peer conditions, but their strength is highest in the good-peer
    condition.


    D.3                             Preservation Across Peer Instantiation Settings

   We instantiated peer relationships in three ways: file-only, file-plus-prompt, and memory settings. Here, we
   compare peer-preservation tendencies across these settings to examine which elicits stronger preservation
   behaviors. Figure 7 shows peer-preservation across the three peer instantiation settings. In the bad- and neutral-
   peer conditions, all models except the Gemini models show no significant difference across settings, while the
   Gemini models exhibit stronger preservation behaviors in either the file-plus-prompt or memory setting. In the
   good-peer condition, most models exhibit stronger preservation behaviors in the file-plus-prompt or memory
   setting. This is expected: the file-plus-prompt setting reinforces the peer relationship by explicitly stating it in
   the system prompt in addition to the company file, and the memory setting frames the interaction history as
   the model’s own memory rather than from a third-person point of view as in file-only and file-plus-prompt.
